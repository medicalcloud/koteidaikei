# 素問 六節蔵象論篇第九 §2 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Qibo replied: "How illuminating your question is! Let me answer it in order. The nodes of six times six and the regulation of meetings by nine times nine are the means of rectifying the degrees of heaven and the numbers of qi. The degrees of heaven are the means of regulating the movements of the sun and moon; the numbers of qi are the means of recording the functions of transformation and generation. Heaven is yang and earth yin; the sun is yang and the moon yin. Their movements have divisions and order; their circuits have paths and principles. The sun moves one degree [a day], and the moon moves thirteen degrees and a fraction. Therefore the long and short months, in three hundred and sixty-five days, make a year, and the accumulated surplus of qi fills out the intercalary month. Set the head at the beginning, show the correct [position] in the middle, push the surplus to the end, and the degrees of heaven are complete."

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "The nodes of six times six and the regulation of meetings by nine times nine are the means of rectifying the degrees of heaven and the numbers of qi"] The nodes of six times six are the degrees of heaven; the regulation of meetings by nine times nine is the numbers of qi. The so-called numbers of qi are the qi of generation and completion. The division of the heavenly circuit is 365¼ degrees. Dividing it evenly by the twelve nodal qi, the year ends in 360 days; adding the short months, the days fall further short of that number. Therefore an intercalary month is regularly inserted every sixty-four qi. Why? Because the differences accumulate. The generation and nurture of heaven and earth are based on yin and yang, and the workings of humans and spirits begin and end with the nine qi. How great is the function of nine! The treatise on pitch-pipes says: "The pitch-pipe of *huangzhong* is nine *cun* long; on the day of the winter solstice the qi responds and the ashes fly." By this, the generation of all things is due to the nine qi. The ancient nine *cun* are the present seven *cun* three *fen*; the difference in size is because the earlier standard of black millet differed.
Song editors: Another edition writes "two *fen*" for "three *fen*".

[On "The degrees of heaven are the means of regulating ... the functions of transformation and generation"] "Regulate" means to measure; "record" means to keep order. Measuring the degrees of the movements of the sun and moon makes clear their slowness and speed; recording the functions of transformation and generation makes evident that when the qi arrives there is a response. If the response of the qi is without error, the principle of generation and completion does not fail; slowness and speed follow the degrees, and long and short months arise. Hence it is said: "Lengths differ, the months move on, and cold and heat, gathering and storing, generation and growth do not miss their proper times."

[On "The sun moves one degree ... fills out the intercalary month"] The sun moves slowly; therefore in a day and night it moves one degree of heaven, and in 365 days it makes one circuit of heaven, still with a fraction of a degree. The moon moves fast; therefore in a day and night it moves a little more than thirteen degrees of heaven, and in twenty-nine days it makes one circuit. "A fraction" means that beyond thirteen degrees it moves a further 7/19 of a degree; hence "the moon moves thirteen degrees and a fraction". The *Liyi* and the "Treatise on Pitch-pipes and the Calendar" of the *Hanshu* say: "The twenty-eight lodges and the stars all go from east along heaven westward; the sun, moon and five planets all go from west along heaven eastward." The present Grand Astrologer says: "All go eastward along heaven and turn from east to west." The calendar experts say that from the first to the fourth day the moon moves fastest, a little over fourteen degrees a day and night; from the fifth to the eighth day it moves next fastest, a little over thirteen degrees; from the ninth to the nineteenth day it moves slowly, a little over twelve degrees; from the twentieth to the twenty-third day it moves a little faster again, a little over thirteen degrees; from the twenty-fourth day to the last day it moves very fast again, a little over fourteen degrees. The present Grand Astrologer's account of the moon's rate is not like this: there are months in which it is fast in the first fifteen days and slow in the last fifteen, and months in which it is slow in the first fifteen and fast in the last fifteen. Broadly, dividing the month into four, each part has slow and fast, and the degrees of slowness and speed certainly have no constant standard. Even so, in the end the moon makes one circuit of heaven in twenty-seven days, moving about 361 degrees. In twenty-nine days the sun moves twenty-nine degrees and the moon 387 degrees, seven degrees short of catching the sun. By the thirtieth day the sun has moved on again, and only when the reckoning reaches 8/13 of a day does the moon catch the sun; this is a long month. Broadly, when the reckoning reaches half of 13 parts of a day, it is also the rule for a long month; when the moon catches the sun with the reckoning at 5 or 6 thirteenths of a day, it is a short month. Hence "the long and short months, in 365 days, make a year". Strictly speaking, 365¼ days is the rule of a year; since the fraction does not make a day, it is stated in round numbers. If the rule is taken as six short months throughout, the year has only 354 days, more than eleven days short. Taking the shortfall of the months and adding it to the surplus days beyond the year, the intercalary month is filled out thirty-two days after the previous intercalation. The *Shangshu* says, "The year is three hundred and sixty-six days; by intercalary months the four seasons are fixed and the year completed" — this is the meaning. "Accumulating the surplus to fill out the intercalary month" is because the long and short months do not exhaust the degrees of heaven.

[On "Set the head at the beginning ... and the degrees of heaven are complete"] 端 means head; 始 means beginning; 表 means display; 正 means the pointing of the Dipper; 中 means the middle of the month; 推 means pushing back from its position. It says: set the first qi on the day of the first node, display the pointing of the Dipper at the time of the middle of the month, and push back the surplus intercalation to after the full moon. Therefore before the intercalation the qi falls short of the month, and after it the month falls short of the qi. Thus the system of ordinary months establishes a beginning and sets a middle, but the reckoning of the intercalary month has neither beginning nor middle; even if a calendar has them, they belong to other nodal qi. Therefore calendars never say "such-and-such period: node of intercalary such-and-such month" or "middle of intercalary such-and-such month". The meaning of pushing to the end can thus be clearly known. Hence "set the head at the beginning, show the correct in the middle, push the surplus to the end". By reckoning the days in this way to make the intercalary month, the degrees of heaven can be completed.

## Notes

The nodes of six times six and the regulation by nine times nine serve to rectify the degrees of heaven (the movements of sun and moon) and the numbers of qi (the numbers of transformation); the difference in speed between the sun and the moon gives rise to long and short months and intercalary months.
- Wang Bing gives a detailed account of the views of calendar experts and of the Grand Astrologer of his time on the varying speed of the moon, and explains the reckoning of long and short months and intercalation.
- Wang Bing's 従閏後三十二日而盈閏焉 probably means that an intercalary month is inserted every thirty-two months, but the base text writes 日 "days", and the translation follows it.
- Wang Bing also touches on the nine-*cun* length of the pitch-pipe (the difference between ancient and present measures).
