# 素問 脈解篇第四十九 §4 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

In the greater yin, what is called distension: the greater yin is *zi*; in the eleventh month the qi of all things is stored within; hence it is called distension. What is called rising to the heart as belching: yin is abundant and rises to the *yangming*; the *yangming* network vessel belongs to the heart; hence it is called rising to the heart as belching. What is called vomiting on eating: things are abundantly full and overflow upward; therefore there is vomiting. What is called feeling relieved as if [the illness] declined on passing stool and wind: in the twelfth month yin qi descends and declines, and yang qi is about to emerge; hence it is called feeling relieved as if declined on passing stool and wind.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "In the greater yin, what is called distension ... hence it is called distension"] Yin qi is greatly abundant and the greater yin begins at *zi*; hence "*zi*". Its vessel enters the abdomen, belongs to the spleen and connects with the stomach; therefore there is distension.

[On "What is called rising to the heart as belching ... as belching"] According to the *Lingshu jing*, the course of the foot *yangming* nowhere reaches the heart. The account of the greater yin vessel says, "A branch goes again from the stomach up through the diaphragm and pours into the heart." By rule, this network vessel should be taken as the *yangming* network vessel.
Song editors: On examination, Mr Wang says the course of the foot *yangming* nowhere reaches the heart. According to the *Jiayi jing*, the *yangming* vessel communicates upward with the heart, follows the throat and emerges at the mouth; it is fitting that the classic says the *yangming* network vessel belongs to the heart and produces belching. How could Mr Wang say there is none?

[On "What is called vomiting on eating ... there is vomiting"] Because its vessel belongs to the spleen, connects with the stomach, rises through the diaphragm and runs alongside the throat.

## Notes

The symptoms of greater yin illness explained by the state of yin and yang in the eleventh (*zi*) and twelfth months.
- 流住 in the Song editors' note is presumably an error for 流注.
