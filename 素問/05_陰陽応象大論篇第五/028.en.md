# 素問 陰陽応象大論篇第五 §28 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Those good at diagnosis examine the complexion and press the pulse, first distinguishing yin and yang; they scrutinize the clear and turbid and so know the regions; they look at the breathing and listen to the voice and so know what is suffered; they observe the weight, beam, compass and square and so know what governs the illness; they press the *chi* and *cun* and observe whether [the pulse] is floating, sunken, slippery or rough, and so know where the illness arises. Treating thereby, there is no fault; diagnosing thereby, nothing is missed.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "Those good at diagnosis ... first distinguishing yin and yang"] Distinguishing the yang, one knows the place of the illness; distinguishing the yin, one knows the time of death or life.

[On "they scrutinize the clear and turbid and so know the regions"] This means examining the complexion — green, red, yellow, white, black. "Regions" means the positions of the zang and fu where one can divine and observe.

[On "they look at the breathing ... and so know what is suffered"] This means listening to the voice — *gong*, *shang*, *jue*, *zhi*, *yu*. "Looking at the breathing" means observing whether the breaths are long or short.

[On "they observe the weight, beam, compass and square and so know what governs the illness"] "Weight" means the counterweight of a steelyard; "beam" means the beam of a balance; "compass" means a round form; "square" means a square image. The weight is used to examine centre and outside; the beam to fix high and low; the compass to show softness and emptiness; the square to make clear strength and abundance. The *Maiyao jingwei lun* (chapter 17) says that spring corresponds to the compass, meaning the yang qi is soft; summer corresponds to the square, meaning the yang qi is abundant and strong; autumn corresponds to the beam, meaning yin rises and yang descends so that the qi has high and low; and winter corresponds to the weight, meaning the yang qi is below. Therefore in the practice of those good at diagnosis all of these must appear. "What governs" means, in correspondence with the qi of the four seasons, whether the illness arising from what governs is high or low, central or outer.

[On "they press the *chi* and *cun* ... and so know where the illness arises, treating thereby"] Floating, sunken, slippery and rough are all pulse images. A floating pulse floats under the hand; a sunken pulse is obtained only by pressing; a slippery pulse comes and goes easily; a rough pulse comes and goes with difficulty. Therefore one scrutinizes the *chi* and *cun*, observes floating and sunken, knows where the illness arises, and treats it.
Song editors: The *Jiayi jing* reads "knowing where the illness lies, treating thereby, there is no fault", continuing the two characters "no fault" below into this phrase.

[On "no fault; diagnosing thereby, nothing is missed"] If whether there is fault or not is always known by diagnosis, there is no error or omission in what is treated.

## Notes

The methods of one good at diagnosis: distinguishing yin and yang by complexion and pulse, knowing the regions by clear and turbid, knowing what is suffered by breathing and voice, knowing what governs the illness by the weight, beam, compass and square (the seasonal pulses), and knowing where the illness arises by whether the *chi* and *cun* pulses are floating, sunken, slippery or rough.
- "Weight, beam, compass and square": Wang Bing explains them as the images of the seasonal pulses, citing the *Maiyao jingwei lun* (chapter 17): spring corresponds to the compass, summer to the square, autumn to the beam and winter to the weight.
- Punctuation: the base text and Wang Bing break after 而知病所生以治, taking 无過以診則不失矣 as the next phrase; according to the Song editors, the *Jiayi jing* reads 知病所在以治則无過. The punctuation here, 以治无過，以診則不失矣, is chosen for readability.
- Daizhige (*Chongguang buzhu Huangdi neijing suwen*) has an extra 声 before 音声.
