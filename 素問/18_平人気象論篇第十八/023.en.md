# 素問 平人気象論篇第十八 §23 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

When a normal spleen pulse comes, it is harmonious and soft, each beat distinct, like a chicken treading the ground; this is called a normal spleen. In late summer the stomach qi is the root. When a sick spleen pulse comes, it is in excess, full and rapid, like a chicken lifting its feet; this is called spleen illness. When a dying spleen pulse comes, it is sharp and hard like a crow's beak, like a bird's spur, like a leaking roof, like flowing water; this is called spleen death.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "When a normal spleen pulse comes ... a normal spleen"] It means the pulse comes with beats rapid and distinct, slow and fast harmonious and regular.

[On "In late summer the stomach qi is the root"] With little stomach qi the pulse is in excess and rapid.

[On "When a sick spleen pulse comes ... spleen illness"] With little stomach qi the pulse is in excess and tense. "Lifting its feet" means like a running chicken lifting its feet.
Song editors: Yueren takes this as heart illness.

[On "sharp and hard like a crow's beak"] Song editors: The *Qianjin fang* writes "like a chicken's beak".

[On "like a bird's spur, like a leaking roof, like flowing water; this is called spleen death"] A crow's beak and a bird's spur mean sharp and hard; flowing water and a leaking roof describe how it arrives: flowing water means arriving evenly without drumming; a leaking roof means beating at times and stopping again.

## Notes

The normal, sick and dying forms of the spleen pulse.
- The base's 啄 "peck" is emended to 喙 "beak". Wikisource (the plain text) and three Daizhige texts all read 喙, and the *Taisu* and the *Leijing* read 如鳥之喙. Wang Bing's note also explains with 喙 ("a crow's beak and a bird's spur mean sharp and hard"), and it pairs with 如鳥之距, so the graphically similar 啄 is taken as an error for 喙. 烏 "crow" is written 鳥 "bird" in the *Taisu*, the *Leijing* and the Daizhige plain text, but Wang Bing's note also writes 烏, so it is kept. According to the Song editors, the *Qianjin fang* reads 如鶏之喙.
- According to the Song editors, Yueren (the *Nanjing*) takes the ill spleen pulse as heart illness.
