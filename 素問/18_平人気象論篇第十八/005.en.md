# 素問 平人気象論篇第十八 §5 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

In spring, with stomach [qi] and slightly wiry, it is called normal; more wiry and less stomach, it is called liver illness; only wiry without stomach, it is called death; with stomach and hair-like, it is called autumn illness; markedly hair-like, it is called present illness. The true qi of the zang disperses in the liver; the liver stores the qi of the sinews and membranes.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "In spring, with stomach [qi] and slightly wiry, it is called normal"] It means slightly resembling wiry, not that it is a faint pulse and wiry. Hooked, soft and weak, hair-like and stony have the same sense.

[On "more wiry and less stomach ... only wiry without stomach, it is called death"] It means tense and increasingly strong, like a newly strung bowstring.

[On "with stomach and hair-like, it is called autumn illness"] Hair-like is the autumn pulse, the qi of metal.

[On "markedly hair-like, it is called present illness"] Wood receives the evil of metal; therefore the illness is present.

[On "The true qi of the zang disperses in the liver ..."] It resembles the dispersal of yang qi; therefore the true qi of the zang disperses. The *Zangqi fashi lun* (chapter 22) says, "The liver desires to disperse; quickly eat acrid to disperse it" — this takes [the course of] following its qi.

## Notes

The spring pulse in relation to stomach qi: with stomach qi and slightly wiry is normal; too wiry is illness; wiry without stomach qi is death. If the pulse of the controlling season (autumn, hair-like) appears, illness comes in autumn; if markedly so, illness is present now. Units 005 to 009 treat the five seasons in the same form.
- Wang Bing says 微弦 means "slightly resembling wiry", not a pulse that is both faint and wiry.
