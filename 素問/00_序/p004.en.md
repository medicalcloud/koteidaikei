# 素問 序 §4 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Releasing bonds and escaping hardship, preserving the true and guiding qi, rescuing the common people into benevolent longevity, and aiding the weak and feeble to find peace: these cannot be achieved except by the way of the three sages. Kong Anguo, prefacing the *Book of Documents*, says, "The books of Fuxi, Shennong and the Yellow Emperor are called the Three Mounds; they speak of the great Way." The bibliographical treatise of Ban Gu's *History of the Han* says, "*Huangdi neijing*, eighteen fascicles." The *Suwen* is nine fascicles of that classic, and with the nine fascicles of the *Lingshu* it makes up that number. Though the years passed and dynasties changed, the transmission and reverence [of the learning] survived; but, fearing it might reach the wrong people, they sometimes concealed parts; so the seventh fascicle was kept by a master, and what is followed today is only eight fascicles. Yet its text is concise, its meaning broad, its principles profound and its import deep. The images of heaven and earth are distinguished, the signs of yin and yang arrayed, the causes of change displayed, the omens of death and life made plain. Without consultation, far and near agree; without agreement, the hidden and the manifest accord. Examining its words, they have proof; testing them in practice, they do not err. Truly it may be called the source of the utmost Way and the beginning of caring for life. Even if one's heavenly faculties are quick and one's subtle understanding reaches the mysterious, so that one's accomplished plans belong to innate knowledge, still one's standards depend on exegesis; never has anyone walked without a path or gone out without a door. But by applying one's mind to refined study, probing the subtle and seeking the hidden, if one's understanding should match the true essentials, one no longer sees the whole ox; hence whatever one undertakes succeeds, as if ghosts and spirits gave hidden aid, and outstanding geniuses of their age appear from time to time. Thus Zhou had Master Qin, Han had Master Chunyu, Wei had Masters Zhang and Hua, all of whom obtained this wondrous way. All renewed its use day by day and greatly aided the multitudes; flowers and leaves flourished in succession, and reputation matched reality. This shows the eminence of the teaching, and also the help of heaven. I, Bing, longed for the Way from my youth and early loved nurturing life. Fortunately I encountered the true classic and took it as my model; but the current editions are full of errors: chapter titles are duplicated, the order is confused, text and meaning are far apart, it is hard to put into practice and hard to understand. With the passing of years, errors have been perpetuated. Sometimes one chapter appears twice under two separate names; sometimes two discussions are merged under one title; sometimes a separate chapter title is set up before the dialogue is finished; sometimes missing slips are not written and it is said they were lost in the past. *Hejing* is duplicated and placed at the head of *Zhenfu*; *Fangyi* is merged into the chapter on cough; *Xushi* is separated to make *Nicong*; *Jingluo* is merged to make *Lunyao*; *Pibu* is divided to make *Jingluo*; *Zhijiao* is pushed back to put *Zhen* first; such cases are innumerable. Now if one would climb Mount Dai, what can one do without a path? If one would reach Fusang, without a boat one cannot get there. So I diligently sought far and wide, and also 井 [found] the right person; after twelve years I reached the essentials of principle, consulted on gains and losses, and deeply fulfilled my long-held wish. At that time, in the study of Master Guo, I received the secret copy of my late teacher Master Zhang; its text was clear and its meaning complete, and by examining everything against it, all my doubts melted like ice. Fearing it might be scattered among later learners and the line of masters cut off, I composed annotations to hand it down imperishably; together with the fascicles long kept, I combined eighty-one chapters in twenty-four fascicles into one work. My hope is only that it will explore the end and clarify the beginning, seek the notes and reconcile them with the classic, enlighten the ignorant, and proclaim the utmost principles. Where slips were missing and text broken so that the meaning did not connect, I searched the classic and discussions for what was there and moved it to fill the place. Where chapter titles had fallen away or were incomplete and the matter referred to was unclear, I weighed the intent and added characters to make the meaning clear. Where chapters and discussions were merged, their meanings unrelated and their titles missing, I divided them by subject and gave separate titles at the heads of chapters. Where the courtesies of ruler and minister in questions and requests were amiss, I examined their rank and supplemented to clarify the meaning. Where misplaced slips and fragmentary text were duplicated, I examined their import and cut away the superfluous to keep the essentials. Where the words and principles were secret and hard to explain roughly, I separately composed the *Xuanzhu* to set out the way. All characters I added are written in red, so that the present and the ancient are always distinguished and the characters not mixed. I hope that it will make the sage's meaning clear and manifest and spread the mysterious words, like the ranked constellations hanging high with Kui and Zhang in order, like a deep spring pure and clear in which every fish and shell can be distinguished; that ruler and minister will have no 天 [untimely] death, that barbarian and Chinese alike may hope for long life; that practitioners make no errors and learners understand; that the utmost Way spreads and its fine sound continues; and that after a 干 [thousand] years people will know the boundless compassion of the great sage. Preface written in the first year of Baoying of the Great Tang, the year being *renyin*.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "The *Suwen* is nine fascicles of that classic, and with the nine fascicles of the *Lingshu* it makes up that number"] Song editors: On examination, Wang's statement is presumably based on Huangfu Shi'an's preface to the *Jiayi jing*, which says, "The *Qilüe* and the bibliographical treatise list the *Huangdi neijing* in eighteen fascicles. Now there are the *Zhenjing* in nine fascicles and the *Suwen* in nine fascicles, eighteen in all; this is the *Neijing*." Hence Wang followed and used it. Also, the nine fascicles apart from the *Suwen* are simply called *Jiujuan* by Zhang Zhongjing of the Han and Wang Shuhe's *Maijing* of the Western Jin; Huangfu Shi'an named them *Zhenjing*, also called *Jiujuan*. Yang Xuancao says, "The *Huangdi neijing* is in two cases, each of nine fascicles." According to the *Suishu jingji zhi*, it is called *Jiuling*; Wang Bing named it *Lingshu*.

[On "outstanding geniuses of their age appear from time to time. Thus Zhou had Master Qin"] Song editors: Another edition has "He and Huan".

[On "together with the fascicles long kept, I combined eighty-one chapters in twenty-four fascicles into one work"] Song editors: On examination, the seventh fascicle of the *Suwen* has long been lost. Huangfu Shi'an was a man of the Jin; in his preface to the *Jiayi jing* he says "there are also losses"; the *Qilu* of the Liang recorded in the *Suishu jingji zhi* also says "only eight fascicles survive". Quan Yuanqi was a man of the Sui, and his annotated edition also lacks the seventh. Wang Bing was a man of the Baoying era of the Tang, more than six hundred years after the Ganlu era of Huangfu Mi of the Jin, yet Bing says he obtained the long-kept fascicles himself; we privately doubt this. Looking at the seven chapters *Tianyuan ji dalun*, *Wuyun xing lun*, *Liuwei zhi lun*, *Qijiao bian lun*, *Wuchang zheng lun*, *Liuyuan zhengji lun* and *Zhizhen yao lun*, they occupy four fascicles of the present *Suwen*; their chapters and fascicles are vast, unlike the other chapters and fascicles of the *Suwen*, and what they record hardly connects with the other chapters. We privately suspect that these seven chapters are the text of the *Yinyang dalun*, which Wang took to supply the lost fascicle, just as the *Zhouguan*, having lost the "Winter Offices", was supplemented with the *Kaogong ji*. Also, Zhang Zhongjing's preface to the *Shanghan lun* says he "drew on the *Suwen*, the *Jiujuan*, the *Eighty-one Difficulties* and the *Yinyang dalun*"; that the *Suwen* and the *Yinyang dalun* are two books is very clear. So Wang merged the *Yinyang dalun* into the *Suwen*. In short, the *Yinyang dalun* too is an ancient medical classic, but it is certainly not the seventh fascicle of the *Suwen*.

[On "Where the words and principles were secret and hard to explain roughly, I separately composed the *Xuanzhu* to set out the way"] Song editors: On examination, Wang's *Xuanzhu* has not been handed down in the world. There are now a *Xuanzhu* in ten fascicles and a *Zhaoming yinzhi* in three fascicles, presumably texts attributed to him by later people. Though not Wang's books, they do shed considerable light on the four fascicles from the nineteenth to the twenty-second of the *Suwen*. The *Yinzhi* in three fascicles is closely complementary to what is now called the *Tianyuan yuce*, but differs much from Wang Bing's interpretation.

## Notes

Wang Bing's preface: the transmission of the *Suwen*, the disorder of the editions of his time, and how he obtained his teacher's secret copy, rearranged the text and annotated it, with his editorial principles.
- In the main text, 授尊 is read as 授学, 井 in 井有其人 as 并, 天枉 as 夭枉 and 干載 as 千載, following the readings of other sources shown in the collation notes. 重合経而冠鍼服 is read as "the *Hejing* is duplicated and placed at the head of *Zhenfu*", following Liu Wansu's *Suwen xuanji yuanbing shi*, which quotes it with chapter titles as 重《合經》而冠《鍼服》.
- 考功記 in the Song editors' note is read as 考工記 (*Kaogong ji*).
