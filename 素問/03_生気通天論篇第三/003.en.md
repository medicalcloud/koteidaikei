# 素問 生気通天論篇第三 §3 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

The yang qi is like heaven and the sun. If it loses its place, life is cut short and is not bright. Therefore the movement of heaven must be made bright by the sun. Thus yang, relying on [heaven], rises; it is what defends the exterior. When due to cold, one should be like a turning pivot; if rising and resting are as if startled, the spirit qi floats. When due to summer heat, there is sweating; if agitated, there is panting and loud breathing; if quiet, there is much talking; the body is like burning charcoal, and when sweat comes out [the heat] disperses. When due to dampness, the head feels as if wrapped; if damp-heat is not removed, the large sinews contract and shorten and the small sinews slacken and lengthen; contraction and shortening produce cramping, slackening and lengthening produce limpness. When due to qi, there is swelling; the four supports take each other's place, and the yang qi is exhausted.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "The yang qi is like heaven and the sun ... life is cut short and is not bright"] This clarifies the function of the yang qi mentioned before. It compares a person having yang to heaven having the sun. If heaven loses its place, the sun is not bright; if a person loses its place, the yang is not firm. When the sun is not bright, the realm of heaven is dark; when the yang is not firm, a person's life is cut short.

[On "Therefore the movement of heaven must be made bright by the sun"] This says that human life certainly must rely on its yang qi.

[On "Thus yang, relying on [heaven], rises; it is what defends the exterior"] This clarifies the parts where the yang qi moves and its proper function of supporting and defending the body.

[On "When due to cold ... the spirit qi floats"] "Like a turning pivot" means moving within; "as if startled" means sudden and abrupt. It says that when due to the cold of heaven, one should stay deep within and keep close and secure, like the inner movement of a pivot, and should not vex and disturb the sinews and bones so that the yang qi is discharged through the skin and harmed by cold toxin. If rising and resting are sudden and abrupt, with galloping about and wild abandon, the spirit qi floats and overflows and finds no rest. The *Maiyao jingwei lun* (chapter 17) says: "In winter [the qi] is in the bones; the hibernating insects keep close and secure, and the gentleman stays in his rooms." The *Siqi tiaoshen dalun* (chapter 2) says: "The three months of winter are called closing and storing. Water freezes and the earth cracks; do not disturb the yang." It also says: "Let the will be as if hidden, as if concealed, as if harbouring a private thought, as if already having gained something. Leave the cold and approach the warmth; do not let leak from the skin, so that the qi is not repeatedly snatched away." This is what is meant.
Song editors: Quan Yuanqi's edition writes "connected pivot". Quan Yuanqi says: "The yang qi being settled as if connected to a pivot means it moves while bound [to it]."

[On "When due to summer heat, there is sweating; if agitated ... if quiet, there is much talking"] This is the case where one could not be still and careful, was harmed by cold toxin, and in summer it changed into summer-heat illness. "Agitated" means restless; "quiet" means calm. *He* (喝) means exhaling loudly with a sound. When an illness is due to summer heat, it should be discharged by sweating. If the exterior is not released, the evil heat attacks within, and both interior and exterior are hot; therefore there is agitation, rapid panting and loud exhaling with sound. If there is no agitation, the interior is hot and the exterior cool, and stagnant heat attacks the centre; therefore there is much talking without order. One version writes 鳴 for 喝.

[On "the body is like burning charcoal, and when sweat comes out [the heat] disperses"] This again clarifies the principle that sweating is appropriate. How is one to rescue a body blazing like burning charcoal? It must be by letting sweat come out; then the hot qi disperses. One version writes 燥 for 燔, which is wrong.

[On "When due to dampness, the head feels as if wrapped ... slackening and lengthening produce limpness"] When exterior heat causes illness, it should be discharged by sweating. Instead, one wets the head, as if wrapping it in something damp, hoping to remove the heat. The hot qi is not released, and together with the dampness attacks within. The large sinews receive the heat and contract and shorten; the small sinews get the dampness and are drawn and lengthened. Contracted and shortened, they cramp and do not stretch; drawn and lengthened, they are limp and without strength. 攘 means remove, 緛 means contract, 弛 means drawn out.

[On "When due to qi, there is swelling; the four supports take each other's place, and the yang qi is exhausted"] When there is a habitual qi disorder and damp-heat is added to it, the qi and the damp-heat contend; therefore there is swelling. As the evil qi gradually grows and the correct qi gradually weakens, sinews, bones, blood and flesh in turn take over each other's burden; hence "the four supports take each other's place". When evil replaces the correct and the qi does not spread and communicate, the defence has nothing to follow and soon declines to exhaustion; hence "the yang qi is exhausted". The defence is the yang qi.

## Notes

The yang qi is to a person what the sun is to heaven, and defends the exterior. The passage then describes illnesses in which the yang qi is harmed by four causes: cold, summer heat, dampness and qi.
- "Like a turning pivot": Wang Bing explains that in cold weather one should stay within and keep close, like a pivot moving within. According to the Song editors, Quan Yuanqi's edition writes "connected pivot".
- "When due to dampness, the head feels as if wrapped": Wang Bing takes this as the result of the mistaken treatment of wetting the head to cool a fever, so that damp-heat attacks within. Another reading takes it as the head feeling heavy as if wrapped when struck by dampness.
- "The four supports take each other's place": Wang Bing takes the four as sinews, bones, blood and flesh; another reading takes them as the four limbs.
- Daizhige writes 軟 for 緛 and 馳 for 弛 in some places.
