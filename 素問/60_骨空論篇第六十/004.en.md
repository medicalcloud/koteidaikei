# 素問 骨空論篇第六十 §4 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

For mouse fistula with cold and heat, needle Hanfu in return; Hanfu is at the joint attached to the outside of the knee, needled into the *ying*. To take [points] on the upper outer knee, have [the patient] kneel in a bow; to take the sole of the foot, have them kneel. The *ren* vessel starts below Zhongji, goes up to the pubic hairline, follows the inside of the abdomen, goes up past Guanyuan, reaches the throat, goes up the chin, follows the face, and enters the eyes. The *chong* vessel starts at Qijie, runs alongside the lesser yin channel, ascends flanking the navel, and reaches the chest, where it disperses. When the *ren* vessel is ill, men have internal knotting and the seven *shan*; women have vaginal discharge and conglomerations and gatherings. When the *chong* vessel is ill, there is counterflow of qi and internal urgency. When the *du* vessel is ill, the spine is stiff and arches back. The *du* vessel starts at the center of the bone below the lower abdomen; in women it enters and connects to the *ting* orifice; that orifice is the end of the urinary orifice. Its network vessel follows the genitals, joins at the perineum, wraps behind the perineum, branches off and wraps around the buttocks, reaching the lesser yin and the network vessel in the middle of the greater yang and joining with them; the lesser yin goes up the posterior edge of the inner thigh, pierces the spine, and connects to the kidneys. [Another course,] with the greater yang, starts at the inner canthus of the eye, goes up the forehead and crosses at the vertex, enters and networks the brain, comes back out and branches down the nape, follows the inside of the shoulder blade, flanks the spine to the lumbus, enters and follows the paraspinal muscles, and networks the kidneys. In men it follows the penis down to the perineum, the same as in women. The branch that goes straight up from the lower abdomen pierces the center of the navel, goes up through the heart, enters the throat, goes up the chin, encircles the lips, and goes up to connect to the center below the two eyes. When this produces illness, [qi] rushes up from the lower abdomen to the heart with pain, there is no passing of stool or urine, and this is *chong shan*. In women there is infertility, urinary block, hemorrhoids, incontinence and dry throat. When the *du* vessel produces illness, treat the *du* vessel; treatment is on the bone, and in severe cases at the *ying* below the navel. For rising qi with sound, treat the center of the throat, the one in the middle of Quepen. For illness rushing up to the throat, treat its *jian*; *jian* is above, flanking the chin.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "needle Hanfu in return; Hanfu is at the joint attached to the outside of the knee, needled into the *ying*"] Between the bones on the outside of the knee. It is the place of bending and stretching, which cold qi tends to strike; hence the name Hanfu ("cold residence"). 解 means the bone joint; 営 means needling deep and surely hitting its *ying*.

[On "To take [points] on the upper outer knee ... have them kneel"] Taking it with a bow opens the hollow of the knee point; taking it kneeling fixes deeply the hollow of the sole.

[On "goes up past Guanyuan, reaches the throat, goes up the chin, follows the face, and enters the eyes"] Song editors: The *Nanjing* and *Jiayi jing* lack the six characters 上頤循面入目.

[On "The *chong* vessel starts at Qijie, runs alongside the lesser yin channel"] Song editors: The *Nanjing* and *Jiayi jing* have *yangming* [for lesser yin].

[On "ascends flanking the navel, and reaches the chest, where it disperses"] The *ren* and *chong* vessels are both extraordinary vessels. The *ren* vessel ascends along the center of the navel; the *chong* vessel ascends on both sides flanking the navel. Zhongji is four body-*cun* below the navel. "Below Zhongji" means that it ascends from Zhongji inside the lower abdomen, comes out at the hairline and goes up; it does not mean that it originally starts there. Guanyuan is three body-*cun* below the navel. Qijie is a point name, on both sides of the hairline, one body-*cun* above the groin. "The *chong* vessel starts at Qijie" likewise means it travels together with the *ren* vessel from inside the lower abdomen and on reaching here follows the abdomen. How do we know? The *Zhenjing* says: "The *chong* vessel is the sea of the twelve channels; with the network vessel of the lesser yin it starts below the kidneys and emerges at Qijie." It also says: "The *chong* and *ren* vessels both start in the uterus, go up along the inside of the spine, and are the sea of the channels and network vessels. Their floating, outer parts follow the abdomen, each meeting at the throat, and branch to network the lips and mouth. When blood and qi are abundant, the skin is hot; when blood alone is abundant, it seeps into the skin and produces fine hair." From this it is clear that the *ren* and *chong* vessels ascend from inside the lower abdomen to below Zhongji and inside Qijie.
Song editors: Qijie duplicates the notes in the *Qifu lun*, *Ci re pian*, *Shuire xue pian*, *Ci jin lun* and elsewhere; the wording differs but the location does not; it is fully annotated in the *Qifu lun*.

[On "When the *du* vessel is ill, the spine is stiff and arches back"] The *du* vessel is also an extraordinary vessel. The *ren*, *chong* and *du* vessels are one source branching into three; hence the classics sometimes call the *chong* vessel the *du* vessel. How is this shown? Now the *Jiayi* and the old *Jingmai liuzhu tujing* call the part of the *ren* vessel that follows the back the *du* vessel, and the part going straight up from the lower abdomen the *ren* vessel, also calling it the *du* vessel. They merely give separate names by the yin and yang of back and abdomen. Because the *ren* vessel goes up from the uterus past the girdle vessel and through the navel, illness in men is internal knotting and the seven *shan*, and in women vaginal discharge and conglomerations. Because the *chong* vessel ascends flanking the navel alongside the lesser yin channel to the chest, its illness is counterflow of qi and internal urgency. Because the *du* vessel ascends along the inside of the spine, its illness is a stiff spine arching back.

[On "The *du* vessel starts at the center of the bone below the lower abdomen; in women it enters and connects to the *ting* orifice"] "Starts" does not mean first arises, just as the *ren* and *chong* vessels start in the uterus. In fact it starts below the kidneys, reaches the lower abdomen, runs down, and reaches the center of the ring of the transverse bone of the lumbus. "Connects to the *ting* orifice" means the so-called orifice of the front yin, near the *yaolou*; it is so named because the *yinting* connects to and belongs within it.

[On "that orifice is the end of the urinary orifice"] The orifice is the *yaolou*; within the *yaolou*, above it, is the urinary orifice. 端 means the *yinting* is at the upper end of this urinary orifice. And the *du* vessel reaches here from the center of the bone ring.

[On "Its network vessel follows the genitals, joins at the perineum, wraps behind the perineum"] The separate network vessel of the *du* vessel divides from the end of the urinary orifice, each branch going down along the genitals and joining at the perineum. 間 means between the front and back yin. Behind this space they divide again and wrap behind the perineum.

[On "branches off and wraps around the buttocks ... pierces the spine, and connects to the kidneys"] 別 means the separate network vessels divide and each go to the *jiao*. The network vessel of the foot lesser yin goes from the posterior edge of the inner thigh through the spine to connect to the kidneys. The outer branch of the foot greater yang network vessel follows the *huashu*, networks the yang side of the thigh and descends; its middle branch goes down through the buttocks to the popliteal fossa and joins the outer branch. Hence "reaching the lesser yin and the network vessel in the middle of the greater yang and joining; the lesser yin goes up the posterior edge of the inner thigh, pierces the spine and connects to the kidneys".
Song editors: On examination, the character 焦 in 各行於焦 is suspected to be an error.

[On "follows the inside of the shoulder blade, flanks the spine to the lumbus, enters and follows the paraspinal muscles, and networks the kidneys"] It connects with what wraps around the buttocks and ascends.

[On "In men it follows the penis ... the center below the two eyes"] From "with the greater yang, starts at the inner canthus" to "the same as in women" all describe the separate network vessels of the *du* vessel. Its straight course goes from the sacrum up along the inside of the spine to the 鼻人. The branch that goes straight up from the lower abdomen to the center below the eyes runs together with the course of the *ren* vessel, yet is said to be what the *du* vessel connects to. From this, the *ren*, *chong* and *du* vessels differ in name but are the same body.

[On "When this produces illness ... this is *chong shan*"] Looking into what produces this illness, it is precisely the *ren* vessel. That the classic says "this is *chong shan*" makes clear that the *du* vessel is given a different name for its separate governance. Why? If it were one vessel and one qi without different governance of yin and yang, the person with this illness should have pain in both heart and back; why would it only rush to the heart and become *shan*?

[On "In women there is infertility, urinary block, hemorrhoids, incontinence and dry throat"] This too is because the *chong* and *ren* vessels together go up from the lower abdomen to the throat, and the *du* vessel follows the genitals, joins at the perineum, wraps behind the perineum and branches around the buttocks; hence infertility, urinary block, hemorrhoids, incontinence and dry throat. It is called the *ren* vessel because women obtain it to 任養 (conceive and nourish); hence the classic says "in women there is infertility". It is called the *chong* vessel because its qi rushes up; hence the classic says "[qi] rushes up from the lower abdomen to the heart with pain". It is called the *du* vessel because it governs the sea of the channels. Because of these three functions there is one source with three branches; the classics sometimes call them by a common name and seem to cite each other mistakenly. Hence the following text says:

[On "When the *du* vessel produces illness, treat the *du* vessel; treatment is on the bone, and in severe cases at the *ying* below the navel"] This too is properly the territory of the *ren* vessel; that the three vessels *chong*, *ren* and *du* differ in name but are the same body is again clear. "On the bone" means the Qugu point, above the transverse bone of the lumbus in the middle of the hairline, the meeting of the *ren* vessel and foot *jueyin*; one may needle one and a half body-*cun*; for moxibustion, three cones. "Below the navel" means the Yinjiao point, one body-*cun* directly below the navel, the meeting of the *ren* vessel and *yinchong*; one may needle eight body-*fen*; for moxibustion, five cones.

[On "For rising qi with sound, treat the center of the throat, the one in the middle of Quepen"] 中 means the middle between the two Quepen. It is the Tiantu point, four body-*cun* below the laryngeal prominence of the neck, in the central hollow, the meeting of the *yinwei* and *ren* vessels; take it with the needle held low; one may needle one body-*cun* and retain seven breaths; for moxibustion, three cones.

[On "For illness rushing up to the throat, treat its *jian*; *jian* is above, flanking the chin"] The *yangming* vessel gradually (漸) goes up the chin and encircles the lips, so the place flanking the chin is named *jian*. This is Daying. Daying is in the depression one *cun* three *fen* in front of the bone at the angle of the jaw, at the pulsing vessel, where the vessel qi of the foot *yangming* issues; one may needle three body-*fen* and retain seven breaths; for moxibustion, three cones.

## Notes

The needling of mouse fistula, and the courses, illnesses and treatment of the *ren*, *chong* and *du* vessels. Wang Bing treats the three as "one source with three branches", "different in name, the same body".
- 営 in 寒府在附膝外解営 is read with Wang Bing as needling deep into the *ying*. Some attach 営 to the next phrase as 営取.
- In Wang Bing's note, 滑枢 is presumably an error for 髀枢, and 鼻人 is presumably corrupt; the Siku quanshu edition (Daizhige *Huangdi neijing suwen*) also has 滑枢, and the correct characters have not been verified. The Song editors also suspect 焦.
- 陰衝之会 (for Yinjiao) is translated as Wang Bing has it.
