# 素問 上古天真論篇第一 §18 (English translation)

## Translation of the text

Next there were sages. They dwelt in the harmony of heaven and earth and followed the patterns of the eight winds; they kept their cravings moderate amid the world and its customs and had no anger or resentment in their hearts. In conduct they did not wish to leave the world, [*bei fu zhang*], and in action they did not wish to be seen by the common people. Outwardly they did not wear out their bodies with affairs; inwardly they had no troubles of anxious thought. They made tranquillity and contentment their task and self-fulfilment their achievement. Their bodies did not wear out and their essence and spirit did not scatter, and they too could reach a hundred years.

## Translation of Wang Bing's commentary and the Song editors' notes

[On "Next there were sages. They dwelt in the harmony of heaven and earth and followed the patterns of the eight winds"] They match their virtue with heaven and earth, their brightness with the sun and moon, their order with the four seasons, and their good and ill fortune with ghosts and spirits; therefore they are called sages. That they dwell in the pure harmony of heaven and earth and follow the correct patterns of the eight winds is because they wish to nourish what is correct and avoid depleting evils.

[On "they kept their cravings moderate amid the world and its customs and had no anger or resentment in their hearts"] Because the sage's will is deep in the Way, they are moderate in their cravings; because their heart is whole and loves broadly, they have no anger or resentment. Therefore constant virtue does not leave them, and to the end of their lives they are not imperilled.

[On *bei fu zhang*] Song editors: We suspect that the three characters 被服章 are superfluous; they do not connect with the text before and after.

[On "In conduct they did not wish to leave the world ... did not wish to be seen by the common people"] Though the sage, in undertaking affairs and in going and stopping, is always among the customs of the time, their views and conduct simply differ from those customs. Why? Because they value the clarity and stillness that follow the Way. The *Laozi* says: "I alone differ from others, and I value seeking nourishment from the mother." "Mother" is also a figure for the Way.

[On "Outwardly they did not wear out their bodies with affairs; inwardly they had no troubles of anxious thought"] The sage does non-action and makes no-affair their affair; therefore inwardly they have no anxious thought, and outwardly they do not wear out their bodies.

[On "They made tranquillity and contentment their task and self-fulfilment their achievement"] *Tian* means still; *yu* means glad. Following the clarity and stillness of the Way, they move in accordance with their nature; therefore they are glad and self-fulfilled.

[On "Their bodies did not wear out ... they too could reach a hundred years"] Because outwardly they do not wear out their bodies and inwardly they have no anxious thought, their bodies do not wear out and their essence and spirit are kept whole. Because the spirit is guarded and does not leave, their years reach a hundred. This, it seems, comes from keeping their nature whole. Gengsang Chu says: "As to sounds, colours and flavours, the sage takes them if they benefit his nature and casts them away if they harm it. This is the way of keeping one's nature whole." *Bi* means worn out.

## Notes

The third grade, the sage: living amid the world yet moderate in desire, without anger, not wearing out the body with affairs outwardly or troubled by anxious thought inwardly. Neither body nor spirit is harmed, and they can live to a hundred.
- *Bei fu zhang* (被服章): the Song editors suspect these three characters are superfluous, as they do not connect with the surrounding text. The translation leaves them untranslated in brackets. Another reading takes them as "wearing ceremonial robes with insignia".
- The opening of Wang Bing's note draws on the "Wenyan" commentary on the *Qian* hexagram of the *Yi* ("matches his virtue with heaven and earth").
- In Wang Bing's note the base text writes 尓, translated as 爾.
